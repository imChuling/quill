Capture · Preserve · Transform

An expressive renderer must learn two things: how an instrument sounds and how it is played.

Quill keeps them as two separately learned, reusable assets: a lightweight DDSP timbre model and a note-level style library of pitch and energy trajectories. Either asset can be swapped across instruments without retraining.

This page is a listening demo of that claim: three timbres, four style libraries, and your melody rendered on a server. Capturing instruments, training timbres and playing Quill in real time are desktop-only. What differs ↓

Why the split: the two assets the paper describes are learned on the desktop; this page only recombines assets that already exist. That is the part that is cheap to serve and honest to demonstrate.

From the paperthe claim

Timbre model

DDSP, harmonic plus noise, about 500K parameters, 16 kHz audio at 250 fps. It takes explicit f0 and loudness, the same quantities the style library stores, so the two interface without a learned adapter.

Style library

Note-level control trajectories extracted from real performances: 48 frames of pitch deviation and energy per note, an articulation label from the pYIN contour (plain, vibrato, slide) and a 6-D context vector. Rendering retrieves the nearest entry, filtered by label, and time-stretches it to the note.

Evaluation

Cross-rendering across violin, trumpet and guitar passes all 12 source-attribution checks. In a pre-specified listening study (N=19), renders carrying retrieved behavior were rated more human-like by 19/19 and more expressive by 18/19; cross-instrument ABX median accuracy 100%. Both assets also learn from 82 s of rubbed-glass recordings.

Sample-based instruments bake performance micro-dynamics into fixed recordings. Here the performance is an asset of its own. Capturing new instruments, training timbres and playing Quill live are in the desktop app; this page has three timbres, four style libraries and your melody.
